#### **An LLM policy agent on a campus epidemic ABM whose claimed superiority holds only under a self-defined composite metric; the simple threshold baseline contains infections better, the LLM is unnamed, and key baselines and validation are missing.**

Official Reviewby Program Committee eojL01 Sept 2026, 13:54 (modified: 25 Sept 2026, 05:21)Program Chairs, Area Chairs, Senior Program Committee, Program Committee eojL, Authors, Program Committee Submitted[Revisions](https://openreview.net/revisions?id=DXGs61z3d1)

**Paper Summary:**

The paper argues that compartmental epidemic models miss individual heterogeneity and that agent-based models typically execute fixed intervention rules. It proposes CEMAS, coupling an individual-level simulator over a six-layer campus contact network with an extended SEIR natural history to an LLM policy agent that selects a weekly intervention level from a discrete validated policy set. The agent's context combines a structured epidemic observation, a dual-branch memory short-term trajectory compression and retrieval from a frozen offline episode bank via a weighted multi-component similarity \ and injected expert knowledge; JSON outputs pass a constraint/validation layer before execution. The system is calibrated to a documented real university outbreak via Bayesian optimization and evaluated on low/medium/high transmission scenarios against no-intervention and threshold-rule baselines using a composite cost-benefit utility. The LLM agent attains the best composite utility despite slightly higher cumulative infections, mainly by avoiding costly online teaching and switching policies less often; ablations show the memory substantially reduces infections and seed variance, while expert-knowledge injection is assessed only qualitatively.

**Strengths:**

- Sensible closed-loop formulation (Sec. 3, Fig. 2): constraining the LLM to a validated discrete policy set with transition rules is safer than free-form policy output. -Well-specified, leakage-aware memory: the global bank is built offline and frozen during evaluation, and the retrieval similarity is fully written out.

**Weaknesses:**

- Baselines cannot support the claim (Table 1). The only adaptive baseline is one hand-crafted threshold rule — which beats the LLM on infections in every scenario. Missing: an RL policy over the same action space (the canonical comparator, cf. Kompella et al. 2020; Colas et al., EpidemiOptim), a tuned threshold family, a no-memory LLM row in the main table, and comparisons to the LLM-epidemic systems the paper itself cites.
- The winning metric is self-defined. Composite-utility weights are author-chosen with no sensitivity analysis; under a pure infection metric the conclusion flips, and the no-intervention arm scoring identically across scenarios suggests saturating normalization.
- No simulator validation: single-outbreak calibration with no simulated-vs-observed comparison, no fit metric, and no out-of-sample test.

**Justification of Recommendation:**

The four fundamental weaknesses jointly drive the rating: the headline claim rests on an author-weighted metric against one hand-crafted baseline that wins on infection control, in an unvalidated simulator, with an unnamed LLM. The memory mechanism and its ablation are genuinely good but cannot carry acceptance alone. An RL baseline, weight-sensitivity analysis, out-of-sample validation, and full disclosure would each have raised my rating.

**Specific Points of Feedback for Rebuttal:**

1- Which LLM powers the agent, and do the results hold under at least one other model? 2- Can you add an RL baseline on the same policy set and observation, or a threshold family tuned with the same optimization budget? 3- Show how the composite-utility conclusion varies across a reasonable range of weightings i.e. where does the LLM remain superior? 4- Show the calibration fit against observed outbreak data with an error metric, and explain the disagreement between your two tables on the same condition.

**Rating:** 3: Clear rejection

**Confidence:** 3: The reviewer is fairly confident that the evaluation is correct

#### **Promising Framework, but the Evaluation Does Not Yet Establish the Advantage of LLM-Based Control**

Official Reviewby Program Committee SSFu23 Aug 2026, 20:07 (modified: 25 Sept 2026, 05:21)Program Chairs, Area Chairs, Senior Program Committee, Program Committee SSFu, Authors, Program Committee Submitted[Revisions](https://openreview.net/revisions?id=jYQwuDZvsL)

**Paper Summary:**

This paper proposes CEMAS, a campus epidemic simulation framework that combines an individual-level agent-based simulator, a six-layer campus contact network, and an LLM policy agent for adaptive intervention. The LLM uses short-term trajectory memory, retrieval from historical simulation cases, and epidemiological expert knowledge to select policies from a predefined action set. The simulator is calibrated using the Fall-2020 University of Notre Dame COVID-19 outbreak, and experiments compare the proposed method with no intervention and threshold-based control under different transmission scenarios.

**Strengths:**

S1. The paper studies an important and well-motivated problem, namely adaptive epidemic intervention under heterogeneous campus contact patterns.

S2. The simulation framework is relatively comprehensive, modeling six campus contact layers together with testing, tracing, isolation, and online-teaching interventions.

S3. The dual-branch memory and expert-knowledge mechanisms are intuitive, and the ablation studies suggest that they improve the temporal stability of LLM policy decisions.

**Weaknesses:**

W1. Limited external validity. The real outbreak data are mainly used to calibrate the simulator, while policy evaluation is conducted entirely within the same simulator family. Thus, the results demonstrate within-simulator effectiveness rather than real-world or out-of-distribution generalization.

W2. The baselines are relatively weak. The main comparison is against a threshold-rule controller, so it remains unclear whether the observed benefits require an LLM rather than a stronger optimization-, control-, or learning-based policy.

W3. The overall advantage depends strongly on CPBU. The threshold strategy achieves fewer infections, while the LLM obtains higher CPBU because it uses less online teaching and switches policies less often. Since the LLM prompt also explicitly discourages online teaching, the comparison may favor the proposed method unless the baseline is tuned under the same utility objective.

W4. The reported (R_e) values appear inconsistent. Calibration penalizes (R_e) outside ([1.5,3.5]), but the sensitivity analysis reports early (R_e) values of approximately 4.52--6.71. The paper should clarify whether these quantities use different definitions or time windows.

W5. The memory ablation is confounded by intervention duration. With memory, the strictest intervention is maintained for about 37 days rather than 12.6--15.4 days, so the lower infection count may partly result from simply applying stronger control for longer rather than better reasoning alone.

**Justification of Recommendation:**

The paper presents a coherent and interesting framework, and the combination of multilayer epidemic simulation, LLM-based decision making, memory, and expert knowledge is potentially valuable. However, the current evaluation does not yet convincingly establish that the LLM itself provides a clear advantage over stronger non-LLM alternatives, and several key conclusions depend on the chosen utility function and experimental setup. The apparent (R_e) inconsistency and the confounding effect in the memory ablation also require clarification.

**Specific Points of Feedback for Rebuttal:**

Q1. Please clarify the (R_e) definition. Why does calibration strongly penalize (R_e>3.5), while the reported early (R_e) reaches 4.52--6.71?

Q2. How was the threshold baseline tuned? Was it optimized using the same CPBU objective as the LLM policy?

Q3. Please summarize the CPBU weight-sensitivity results, especially whether the LLM remains best when infection control receives a substantially larger weight.

Q4. Please clarify how the global memory bank was constructed. Does it contain trajectories generated under the same parameter settings or scenarios used during evaluation?

Q5. How should the memory-ablation improvement be interpreted? Given that memory also increases high-intensity-control duration to about 37 days, how much of the infection reduction is due to memory reasoning versus longer intervention?

**Rating:** 5: Marginally below acceptance threshold

**Confidence:** 4: The reviewer is confident but not absolutely certain that the evaluation is correct

AI Reviewby AI Reviewer ()27 Jul 2026, 19:59 (modified: 25 Sept 2026, 05:21)Program Chairs, AI Reviewer, Area Chairs, Senior Program Committee, Authors, Program Committee Submitted[Revisions](https://openreview.net/revisions?id=JqE7SRzePt)

**Review:**

**Title:** A Campus Epidemic Transmission Simulation Model Based on Multi-Agent Modeling

**Synopsis of the paper**\
The paper presents CEMAS, an individual-based campus epidemic simulator coupled to an LLM that repeatedly selects mitigation policies. The simulator models heterogeneous contact settings and executable testing, tracing, isolation, and teaching interventions. The policy agent combines recent trajectory summaries, retrieval of similar prior simulations, and epidemiological prompt knowledge to trade epidemic control against intervention burden.

**Summary of Review**\
CEMAS provides a concrete closed-loop interface between a multilayer epidemic simulator and a memory-augmented LLM controller. However, the reported cumulative-infection totals cannot be reconciled with the population information and one-infection-per-agent dynamics presented in the main paper. The overall-utility conclusion additionally depends on a composite metric whose normalization masks substantial epidemiological differences, while the simulator-fidelity and layer-dominance claims lack direct results. Prior adaptive epidemic controllers and memory-based LLM agents also narrow the contribution and motivate stronger comparisons. Finally, the ablations do not isolate the proposed mechanisms from intervention intensity or from one another.

**Strengths**

- The simulator-policy interface is operational rather than advisory. Section 3 and Figures 1–2 map a validated policy identifier to fixed testing, tracing, isolation, and online-teaching parameters, enabling closed-loop policy effects to be evaluated within the same environment.
- The memory design separates within-run temporal continuity from cross-run case reuse. Equations (7)–(11) specify trajectory compression and multimodal similarity retrieval rather than leaving memory as unstructured conversation history, making the proposed mechanism concrete enough for analysis.
- The evaluation considers both epidemiological outcomes and intervention burdens under shared simulation conditions. Tables 1–2 report infections, quarantine, teaching disruption, testing, switching, and control duration over repeated seeds, exposing trade-offs that an infection-only evaluation would conceal.
- Calibration is tied to an observed campus outbreak rather than a wholly synthetic trajectory. The Experimental Setup identifies the Notre Dame daily-diagnosis sequence and the parameters optimized against it, providing a concrete empirical anchor for the scenario analysis.

**Weaknesses**

- The primary outcome is not internally interpretable from the reported model and population. Equation (1) permits only one progression from susceptible to recovered per agent, yet Table 1 reports as many as 51,651 cumulative infections, while the Experimental Setup identifies approximately 12,000 students plus an unspecified number of faculty and staff. Thus, either the simulation contains at least 51,651 agents, “cumulative infections” does not count uniquely infected agents, or the tally conflicts with the stated dynamics; the exact population and counting definition are essential because every headline comparison uses this quantity.
- CPBU does not robustly support the central “better overall utility” conclusion. Table 1 assigns no intervention exactly 0.550 in every scenario despite cumulative infections ranging from 47,718 to 51,651; under Equation (13) and the displayed zero intervention burdens, this implies that its normalized infection cost is fixed at 1. In High, the LLM score of 0.564 is only 0.014 above no intervention, while threshold rules score 0.399 despite reducing infections from 51,651 to 14,763, so the ranking is determined by undisclosed normalization and chosen weights without sensitivity analysis or CPBU uncertainty.
- The evidence does not support the paper’s main simulator-validation claims. The Abstract states that dormitories and classrooms are “consistently the dominant transmission settings,” and the Conclusion says CEMAS “faithfully reproduce[s] campus outbreak dynamics,” but Section 4 shows neither a calibration-fit result nor layer-specific transmission contributions. The reported policy experiments therefore cannot validate either empirical fidelity or setting-level attribution.
- The contribution is insufficiently distinguished from direct technical precedents that are absent from the paper’s references, related work, and tables. Adaptive epidemic planning with reinforcement learning and graph-based epidemic control already exist (Mai et al., 2023; Meirom et al., 2021), while RAFA and DiLu provide feedback- or retrieval-based LLM decision loops (Liu et al., 2024; Wen et al., 2024). The campus-specific combination may remain distinct, but comparison only with a threshold rule does not establish the value of the LLM controller over learned adaptive control.
- The memory ablation confounds the proposed mechanism with substantially greater intervention intensity. Table 2 pairs infection reductions of 28.9% and 25.2% with increases in post-peak strict-control duration from 12.6–15.4 days to approximately 37 days. Without a burden-matched, fixed-duration, or always-high controller, the experiment shows that memory induces prolonged strict control, not that retrieval improves the control–burden trade-off.
- The ablations do not support the claimed components at their stated granularity. Table 2 disables an unspecified “historical decision-memory module” rather than separately testing the short-term and global branches, while Figure 4 reports infection trajectories rather than policy switches or intervention-level variability for the claim that expert knowledge improves “decision stability.” Neither branch-specific value nor policy-level stability is therefore demonstrated.

**Suggestions for Improvement**

- The policy task should be defined in one compact table before the memory architecture. Section 3 introduces the abstract set , and Figure 2 shows levels P1–P5, but the main text does not expose their testing, tracing, isolation, and teaching parameters; a unified observation–action–feedback specification would make the controller’s actual decisions understandable.
- A controller failure analysis would provide information unavailable from aggregate endpoints. Figure 2 includes transition validation and output-consistency checks, but the experiments do not characterize invalid outputs, fallback frequency, misleading retrievals, premature relaxation, or unnecessary escalation.
- Generalization could be studied explicitly as retrieval distribution shift by constructing global memory on one scenario family and evaluating on disjoint transmission, network, or importation regimes. This would distinguish adaptation through reusable experience from interpolation among simulations represented in the memory bank.
- The narrative should separate the simulator contribution from the controller contribution. The technical novelty and ablations center on the policy agent, whereas the title, Abstract, and Conclusion also make broad simulation and empirical-fidelity claims; distinguishing these roles would clarify which evidence supports each contribution.

**Additional Details and Minor Issues**

- Table 1 reports 1.4 online-teaching days for the LLM in High, contradicting the statement that it activates online teaching in no scenario.
- “Daily quarantine” in Table 1 lacks an aggregation unit, preventing interpretation as average census, unique individuals, or person-days.
- Equation (7) requires an explicit empty-history rule because its recency-weight denominator is zero at the first decision cycle.
- Equations (9)–(10) need edge-case rules for zero layer vectors and constant or undersized trend sequences.
- The memory-enabled method has different Medium and High values in Tables 1 and 2 without an identified protocol or seed-set difference.
- Figure 4 uses “theory/notheory,” whereas the text uses “expert knowledge”; consistent labels would remove ambiguity.
